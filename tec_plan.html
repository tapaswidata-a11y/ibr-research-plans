Does ionospheric TEC see an earthquake coming?

Eight papers in your bibliography touch seismo-ionospheric TEC for this region. Five of them make a precursor claim, and each uses a sliding median with a fixed 2σ threshold; none reports how often that threshold fires when no earthquake follows. Without that number the claim is unfalsifiable, which is precisely the criticism the precursor field has faced for forty years. This project supplies the missing number — with a machine-learning detector, and with the alarm scoring that the machine learning does not replace.

01What the eight papers do

A shared recipe, and a shared omission

The regional workflow is remarkably consistent. Take vertical TEC at a station or from a global map; build a running median over the previous 15 or 27 days; flag any hour whose TEC departs from that median by more than about 2σ; discard days when the geomagnetic indices are disturbed; and check whether a flagged day falls within the earthquake preparation zone in the days before a target event.

Two of those are pure methodology and one is explicitly co-seismic — worth separating, because a co-seismic ionospheric disturbance is a well-established physical observation and not in dispute. Acoustic waves from the rupture reach the F region in about eight minutes and are detectable. That is real physics. The precursory claim is a different and far weaker one, and it is the one under test here.

⚡ The omission, stated precisely

A 2σ threshold on a noisy geophysical series fires roughly 5% of the time by construction. Over a decade that is about 180 alarm days. If a handful of them precede earthquakes, the interesting question is not "did TEC anomalies precede earthquakes" — with that many alarms, some inevitably did — but "did they precede earthquakes more often than random alarms of the same rate would". None of the five precursor papers answers that, and two of them do not report an alarm rate at all, which makes the question unanswerable from the published text.

02Making TEC properly

The processing chain, and where it bites you on Linux

Dual-frequency GNSS gives slant TEC almost for free — the ionosphere is dispersive, so the differential delay between the L1 and L2 carriers is proportional to the electron content along the ray path. Everything difficult is in what comes after.

Two routes, and you should run both

Global ionosphere maps (IONEX from CODE, JPL, UPC or ESA, via CDDIS) give you a ready-made VTEC grid, typically 2.5° × 5°, at 1–2 hour cadence, spanning decades. Coarse, but calibrated by people who do this professionally, and the fastest route to a first result. Station RINEX from IGS and the Indian networks gives far finer time resolution and the actual pierce points over your region, at the cost of doing the DCB work yourself.

⚠️ A practical note for your Linux setup

The standard tool in the Indian community — Seemala's GPS-TEC analysis application, currently version 3.5 — is a Windows executable. You are on Linux. Either run it under a VM or Wine, or use a Python route (georinex for RINEX I/O plus your own DCB handling, or GNSSTk/gLAB). Whichever you choose, validate it against IONEX GIMs for the same days before trusting a single anomaly — most reported precursors are smaller than the disagreement between two processing chains.

03The confounders, and why your region is the hardest case

Northeast India sits under the worst possible piece of ionosphere for this work

This deserves stating plainly, because it is the strongest scientific content in the project and it is absent from the regional literature.

The equatorial ionization anomaly (EIA) — the twin bands of enhanced electron density that form either side of the magnetic equator when the daytime fountain effect lifts plasma and lets it diffuse down field lines — puts its northern crest directly across the Indian subcontinent. Your region sits near that crest, and the crest moves: its latitude and strength change with local time, season, solar flux and magnetic activity. The result is day-to-day VTEC variability of tens of percent with no seismic cause whatsoever, which is larger than any anomaly the precursor papers report.

On top of that:

  • Geomagnetic storms produce TEC excursions far larger than any claimed precursor. Screening on Kp and Dst is standard — but the screening thresholds are a free parameter, and moving them moves your results.
  • Solar cycle and F10.7. TEC scales with solar flux, so a fixed σ computed over a multi-year window is non-stationary. The 2012–2018 window used in the regional work spans a solar maximum and a deep solar decline.
  • Equatorial plasma bubbles and scintillation are endemic to this longitude sector after sunset, and they are irregular by nature. Post-sunset "anomalies" in this region need a very good reason not to be bubbles.
  • The preparation-radius convention. The Dobrovolsky relation ρ = 100.43M km gives about 624 km for M 6.5 — a circle large enough to contain a great deal of independent ionosphere and, over a decade in this region, a great many earthquakes. A wider radius mechanically increases your hit rate and your false-alarm rate; only a scored comparison separates the two.

04The missing half: scoring the alarms

This is the contribution — the ML is secondary to it

Anomaly detection produces alarms. An alarm system is evaluated by comparing it against a random alarm system that fires at the same rate. The framework exists, it is standard in earthquake forecast testing, and it has essentially never been applied to regional TEC precursor claims.

Two consequences worth internalising before you start. First, the base rate dominates: target earthquakes are rare, so even a detector with excellent recall will have terrible precision, and quoting accuracy is meaningless. Report the Molchan trajectory, the probability gain and the area skill score. Second, the target catalogue must be declustered — if aftershocks count as separate targets, one lucky alarm before a mainshock scores repeatedly, which is the same double-counting the b-value project has to avoid.

05Where the machine learning belongs

Not as a detector of earthquakes — as a model of the quiet ionosphere

The framing that makes this project defensible: do not train a model to predict earthquakes. Over 25 years you have of order ten target events and roughly 219,000 hourly TEC samples. A classifier trained directly on that imbalance will memorise the positives and learn nothing, and it will do so while producing an impressive-looking accuracy. Train a model to predict TEC instead, and treat the part it cannot predict as the anomaly.

That inverts the usual precursor pipeline in a way that matters: the physics you are trying to remove — solar flux, geomagnetic activity, diurnal and seasonal cycles, the anomaly crest overhead — becomes the model's job, and it is a job the ionospheric community has already solved well.

Layer 1 — forecast the quiet ionosphere

This is a solved and actively published problem, and you should borrow rather than invent. The ingredients are consistent across the literature: a sequence model over past TEC, driven by solar and geomagnetic indices. Inputs: TEC history for the target cell and its neighbours, F10.7 or EUV flux, Kp, Dst, hour of day and day of year encoded cyclically. Target: TEC one to several hours ahead. Architectures, in ascending order of cost: GRU or LSTM on a single cell's series; CNN-BiLSTM when you want spatial context from the surrounding grid; transformer when you want multi-day lead time. Published work exists at every rung, including for storm periods, which is exactly the regime that generates your false alarms.

Layer 2 — turn residual into anomaly

The residual — observed minus predicted TEC — is the quantity to threshold, and it is a far better-defined object than a departure from a 15-day median, because the drivers have already been absorbed. Two disciplines apply. Standardise the residual per cell and per local-time bin, since forecast error is not homogeneous across the anomaly crest or across the day. And estimate the threshold on a training period only, then hold it fixed — a threshold re-fitted on the test period is leakage wearing a lab coat.

Layer 3 — turn anomaly into alarm, then score it

An anomalous cell-hour is not an alarm. Define explicitly how anomalies aggregate into a space–time alarm region — how many cells, over what window, within what radius — because that definition is what the Molchan diagram scores. Declare it before you look, and declutter alarms the same way you decluster the target catalogue, so one long anomaly is not counted as thirty.

⚡ Baselines you have to beat, in order

  • Persistence — tomorrow's TEC equals today's. Embarrassingly strong at short horizons.
  • Climatology — the 15-day sliding median, i.e. the method in the papers you are testing. This is the baseline the project exists to beat or refute.
  • An empirical model — IRI or NeQuick as the "expected" ionosphere. Physics rather than fitting, and it costs you nothing to run.
  • One neuron — logistic regression on a handful of features. Mignan and Broccardo showed a two-parameter logistic regression matching a 13,451-parameter deep network on an aftershock task. If your LSTM cannot beat this, the LSTM is not the finding.

⚠️ Three ways to leak, all of them fatal

  • Normalising before splitting. Computing means or σ over the whole series leaks the test period into training. Fit scalers on training data only.
  • Random splits on a time series. Shuffling hours puts tomorrow in the training set and yesterday in the test set. Split chronologically, always — and keep the pre-event window of every target event out of training entirely.
  • Choosing the window after seeing the result. Fix the pre-event window, the radius and the magnitude threshold in advance. Trying 3, 5 and 7 days and reporting the best is the same error the b-value literature makes, and here it is easier to commit and harder to spot.

ℹ️ The compute is not the constraint

Hourly TEC from 2001 to 2025 is about 219,000 samples per grid cell — a series a GRU will fit on a laptop CPU in minutes, and on your GPU in seconds. Everything expensive about this project is experimental discipline: the splits, the fixed thresholds, the scoring. Budget your time accordingly.

06The protocol

Ordered so that a null result is interpretable — which needs a positive control

The order below is not arbitrary. Steps 03 and 05 are the two that make a negative finding mean something: without a positive control you cannot distinguish "no precursor exists" from "my pipeline does not work", and without a matched random null you cannot say a score is poor rather than merely low.

Phase 1 · Controls & Dual-Processing Setup Steps 01–03
  1. Fix the target catalogue and the alarm rules first

    Magnitude threshold, region, declustering, pre-event window, preparation radius — written down and committed to the repository before the first plot. Use the same regional catalogue as the b-value project so the two studies are commensurable.

  2. Build two independent TEC series

    IONEX global maps over the region for the full period, and station-level VTEC from RINEX for the densest sub-period. Cross-validate them against each other and report the disagreement — that spread is your noise floor, and any claimed anomaly smaller than it is not a measurement.

  3. Positive control: recover a known co-seismic disturbance

    Before testing any precursory claim, confirm your pipeline detects an ionospheric disturbance that is known to exist — the acoustic wave arriving in the F region minutes after a large rupture. Catherine et al. (2017) did this for Gorkha 2015 and Colonna et al. (2026) for Myanmar 2025, both in your library, so you have published targets to reproduce. If this step fails, stop and fix the processing — every later null result would otherwise be uninterpretable.

Phase 2 · Published Baseline & Null Ensemble Steps 04–05
  1. Reproduce the published precursor method exactly

    15-day sliding median, 2σ, storm screening, applied to the five papers that make a precursory claim. Recover their reported anomalies. This is the control condition, and reproducing it is what earns you standing to critique it. Where a paper's parameters are not stated precisely enough to reproduce, record that — it is a finding in itself.

  2. Score that baseline against a matched random null

    Generate a large ensemble — a thousand or more — of random alarm sets that fire at exactly the same rate as the 2σ rule, score each, and plot the resulting distribution. Then place the real detector in it. This is the single figure the field is missing, and you have it before writing any machine learning.

Phase 3 · Quiet-Ionosphere ML & Scoring Steps 06–09
  1. Declare the splits, then never touch them

    Chronological only: an early training period, a middle validation period for model selection, and a late test period opened once. Every target event's pre-event window is excluded from training. Write the dates into the repository at this step, not later.

  2. Fit the driver-aware forecast model

    Train on the quiet period with F10.7, Kp, Dst and time-of-day/year as inputs alongside TEC. Select architecture and hyperparameters on the validation period only. Report its plain forecasting skill first — RMSE against persistence, climatology and an empirical model — because a model that forecasts TEC badly cannot produce meaningful residuals.

  3. Form residual anomalies with thresholds fixed on training data

    Standardise per cell and per local-time bin. The threshold is chosen once, on training data, and then frozen permanently.

  4. Score the ML detector on the same Molchan diagram

    Plot as a trajectory across thresholds rather than a single operating point, against the same random null and alongside the 2σ baseline and the one-neuron model. Three curves on one axis is the paper's main figure.

Phase 4 · Sensitivity & Prospective Release Steps 10–11
  1. Map the sensitivity surface

    Vary radius, pre-event window, magnitude cut and storm screening, and show the score across that grid rather than its best corner. A result that survives only one parameter combination has been found by searching, not by measuring.

  2. Declare a prospective rule and release everything

    Fix the best detector's parameters, state what it would have alarmed on, and run it forward on data acquired after the test period. Publish the anomaly series, the target catalogue and the scoring code, so the next claim in this field can be scored in an afternoon. That harness outlives whichever way your result falls.

07What you publish

Including the outcome most people would rather not have

If the detectors land on the diagonal, you have the first properly scored assessment of seismo-ionospheric precursors for the Indo-Burma region, and a clean statement that the reported anomalies carry no forecasting skill. That is a genuine contribution to a field that badly needs falsifiable results, and it is publishable in a space-physics or natural-hazards venue. It also protects you: several of the eight papers are by groups you may end up collaborating with, so frame it as method, not indictment.

If something beats chance, the bar rises rather than falls — you would need to show robustness across the parameter surface, and ideally a prospective test on data after the training period. That is a much bigger paper, and the scoring machinery you built is what makes the claim credible enough to be believed.

Either way you own the infrastructure: a calibrated regional TEC series, a driver-aware ionospheric model, and a scoring harness. The TEC series alone is reusable for co-seismic ionospheric work — where the physics is solid — including the Myanmar 2025 event that Colonna et al. analysed.
