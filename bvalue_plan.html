Do the b-value anomalies survive a significance test?

Twenty-eight papers in your bibliography derive a Gutenberg–Richter b for Northeast India. Every one of them estimates it by maximum curvature or plain MLE, and not one tests whether the variation it reports is larger than random sampling would produce anyway. Two estimators published since 2021 can settle that. The catalogues are already public, the software is pip install, and the answer is a paper whichever way it falls.

01The claim under test

Two families of assertion, and they fail differently

The regional b-value literature makes two distinct kinds of claim, and it is worth separating them because they need different null hypotheses.

Family A — spatial maps

A grid of b estimated in overlapping cells or circles, then interpreted: low b here means high stress, high b there means a fluid-rich or fractured volume, and the pattern correlates with Bouguer gravity or with a fault trace. The unstated assumption is that the map's structure is real rather than sampling noise.

02Why the standard method cannot answer the question

Three failure modes, none of them the researchers' carelessness

The classical recipe — estimate Mc by maximum curvature, fit b by Aki–Utsu maximum likelihood above it, quote Shi & Bolt uncertainty — is not wrong. It is simply unable to distinguish a real b variation from a sampling fluctuation, for three reasons.

The estimate inherits every error in Mc

b and Mc are entangled: set Mc too low and the missing small events bias b downwards; too high and you throw away the data that constrains it. Maximum curvature is known to underestimate Mc, and a map of b built on a map of Mc inherits every error in the second. When a b-map's structure resembles the network's station distribution, that is the tell.

The pre-mainshock window is transiently incomplete

Short-term aftershock incompleteness is well known — small events are missed while the coda of larger ones saturates the records. The same mechanism operates in any burst of activity, including a foreshock swarm. Missing small events lowers the apparent b. A "precursory b drop" and "a burst of activity that the network could not fully record" produce the same signature, and plain MLE cannot separate them. This is the single most likely explanation for Family B.

Nobody counted the comparisons

A b-map with 200 overlapping cells is 200 opportunities for a fluctuation to look like an anomaly. At the 5% level you expect around ten "significant" cells from pure noise, and because the cells overlap they are correlated, so the extremes clump into patches that look tectonic. No paper in the list corrects for this. It is the central reason a formal test is needed rather than a better colour scale.

03The three estimators you will use

All implemented, tested and installable — you are not writing methods code

b-positive (van der Elst, 2021) takes differences between the magnitudes of successive events and keeps only the positive ones. Because differences of exponentially distributed values follow a Laplace distribution, b follows from a maximum-likelihood fit to those differences. The trick is that a transient loss of small events removes pairs roughly symmetrically and leaves the positive-difference statistic almost intact — it is robust to exactly the incompleteness that Family B is most likely measuring. It remains sensitive to baseline network incompleteness, so it does not free you from Mc entirely.

b-significant (Mirwald, Mizrahi & Wiemer, 2024) answers the question the literature skipped. It is an autocorrelation-based test on a series of b estimates that rejects, or fails to reject, the null hypothesis that the underlying b is constant. Instead of asking "is this cell different from that cell", it asks whether the whole field varies more than chance allows — which is the multiple-comparisons problem handled properly.

Binned MLE with the Tinti–Mulargia correction stays in the workflow as the reference method, because the point of the exercise is comparison. Magnitudes are reported in 0.1 bins and the correction matters at the second decimal — the same decimal at which several of the papers claim a difference.

04Building the catalogue

Two homogenised regional catalogues already exist — and they are in your bibliography

Do not homogenise from scratch. Two published catalogues have done the magnitude conversion work for this exact region, and using them makes your result directly comparable to the papers you are testing:

An Updated and Unified Complete Earthquake Catalog of the Shillong Plateau and Adjoining Regionon your disk Shahabuddin & Mohanty · Seismological Research Letters · 2025 · 10.1785/0220240217 The most recent unified catalogue for the plateau. Its authors also published the 2025 spatio-temporal b mapping you are testing — so using their catalogue removes "different data" as an explanation for any disagreement. That is a deliberate and defensible choice. A unified moment magnitude earthquake catalog for Northeast India Das & Meneses · Geomatics, Natural Hazards and Risk · 2021 Wider regional coverage in Mw, from the group that also published the 2012 homogenisation methodology. Use it as the second, independent catalogue — agreement between the two is itself a result.

Add the international bulletins for completeness at the large end and for the period since those catalogues close: the ISC Bulletin and ISC-GEM for reviewed global data, USGS ComCat for rapid coverage, and India's NCS for the densest small-event coverage on the Indian side. SeismoStats can pull FDSN web-service catalogues directly, which removes a day of scripting.

The decision that changes your answer

Declustering. Removing aftershocks changes b, and every choice — Gardner–Knopoff windows, Reasenberg, nearest-neighbour, ETAS-based stochastic declustering — changes it differently. Do not pick one. Run the entire analysis under at least three declustering choices plus the full catalogue, and report the spread. If a claimed anomaly appears under one declustering and not the others, you have found the actual result.

05The protocol

Fix it in writing before you look at the answers — that is what makes it a test

The value of this study is its discipline. If you tune the cell radius until an anomaly appears or disappears, you have reproduced the problem you set out to diagnose. Write the analysis plan down, timestamp it in the repository, and follow it.

  1. Define the target list first. Extract every specific quantitative claim from the papers in section 01 — cell radius, time window, catalogue, magnitude range, the b values quoted and the difference asserted. This list is fixed before any computation. It is also, by itself, a useful table for the paper.
  2. Assemble two catalogues. The Shillong-region unified catalogue and the NE India Mw catalogue, each extended with ISC/ComCat where they end. Record versions and access dates; catalogues are revised.
  3. Characterise completeness properly. Mc by maximum curvature, goodness-of-fit and b-value-stability, with bootstrap uncertainty — three methods, because the disagreement between them is a quantity you need to report.
  4. Reproduce each claim with the original method. Same cells, same windows, plain MLE. You must be able to recover their numbers before you are entitled to dispute them. Where you cannot reproduce a number, that is a finding to report neutrally.
  5. Re-estimate with b-positive. Same geometry, robust estimator. The difference between the two estimates, cell by cell, is your first figure.
  6. Run b-significant on the field. Spatial and temporal. This is the test the literature is missing, and its output — reject or fail to reject a constant b — is the paper's headline.
  7. Bootstrap the precursor claims. For each Family B event, compute the b time series, then re-compute it thousands of times on catalogues with magnitudes randomly reshuffled in time. How often does a drop as large as the claimed one appear by chance? That single number decides each case.
  8. Repeat everything under each declustering choice and publish the spread, not the most convenient run.
  9. Release the code and the derived catalogues. A methodological correction that cannot be re-run is just another opinion in the literature.

06Every outcome is publishable

Which is why this is rank 01 rather than an interesting idea

Outcome A — most of the anomalies fail

The likeliest result, on the evidence from other regions. You publish a regional methodological correction: which claims survive, which do not, and the corrected b field with honest uncertainty. Uncomfortable to write, straightforward to defend, and it constrains every PSHA in the region — because b feeds directly into recurrence.

Outcome B — some survive

Better. You have then established, with a formal test, that specific structures in the Indo-Burma Ranges carry a genuinely different b. That is a stronger statement than any of the original papers made, and it is the foundation for a physical interpretation rather than a correlation.

Outcome C — the catalogue cannot decide

Also a result, and arguably the most useful one: it quantifies how much better the regional catalogue must be before b-mapping is meaningful at all, which is a direct, costed argument for the deep-learning catalogue at rank 02. The two projects then join into one narrative.

Venue: this is a Seismological Research Letters short paper or a Journal of Seismology article. If a single claim is the focus, SRL and several journals take formal Comments — but a regional re-analysis covering the whole set is a better paper and a less adversarial one.

07How to be fooled

The traps in this specific analysis, in the order you will meet them

  • Mixed magnitude types. mb, ML and Mw in one catalogue produce a kinked FMD and a meaningless b. This is the single most common defect in regional b studies — verify the conversion, don't assume it.
  • Magnitude rounding. Reported magnitudes cluster on 0.1 and, in older bulletins, on 0.5. Without the binning correction b is biased at the second decimal, which is where several disputed differences live.
  • Overlapping windows. Adjacent cells share events, so their b estimates are correlated and the map looks smoother and more structured than the data warrant. Report the effective number of independent samples, not the cell count.
  • Choosing the event after seeing the anomaly. Family B papers select a target earthquake and look backwards. With enough M ≥ 6 events in a seismically active region, some will be preceded by a b drop by chance. The prospective framing — "how often does this precede an event, and how often does it occur with nothing following?" — is the only honest one, and it is the same alarm-scoring logic the TEC project needs.
  • Edge effects. Cells at the catalogue boundary and at the network's margin have both fewer events and higher Mc. Low-b rims around study areas are usually the study area's edge, not a structure.
  • Reading tectonics into σ. With 50 events, the standard error on b is roughly ±0.14 — larger than most of the differences interpreted in the literature. Plot the uncertainty on the same axis as the variation before interpreting anything.
