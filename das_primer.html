Distributed acoustic sensing · a route in

A telecom cable is already a seismic array

DAS pings an ordinary optical fibre with laser pulses and reads the phase of the light that scatters back, turning every few metres of glass into a strain sensor. One interrogator on one dark strand gives you thousands of channels along tens of kilometres — no vaults, no permits per site, no batteries in the field. This is the path from the physics to data you can download this afternoon, and then to fibre in Northeast India.

01What DAS actually measures

Phase-sensitive OTDR — and why a DAS channel is not a geophone

An interrogator fires a coherent laser pulse into one end of the fibre. Glass is never perfectly uniform, so a faint fraction of the light Rayleigh-backscatters from frozen-in density fluctuations all along the cable and returns to the same end. Time-of-flight converts return time into distance: light travels at roughly 2×10⁸ m/s in fibre, so a return arriving 100 µs later came from 10 km down the cable.

When the ground strains a section of fibre, that section stretches, and the optical path length between two scattering points changes. The instrument measures the phase difference between the returns from two points separated by the gauge length L. Divide that change by L and you have the average axial strain — or, differentiated, strain rate — over that segment.

Four consequences you have to design around

  • It is strain, not ground velocity. For a plane wave, strain relates to particle velocity through the apparent phase velocity along the cable: ε = −v / capp. Converting DAS to velocity means assuming or solving for capp — a step with no equivalent in seismometer processing.
  • It is single-component. A channel senses strain along the fibre axis only. There is no vertical, no transverse, no three-component polarisation analysis.
  • Coupling is the whole ball game. Cable cemented or directly buried in soil couples well; cable loose in a conduit slides and under-reads; aerial cable on transmission towers is largely decoupled from the ground.
  • Some channels are simply dead. Rayleigh backscatter amplitude varies randomly along the fibre, so a fraction of channels fade into noise — and which ones can change over hours. Channel QC is a permanent part of the pipeline, not a setup step.

Where the sample rate comes from

You cannot fire the next pulse until the last one has returned, or the returns overlap. That round trip caps the sample rate: fmax = cfibre / 2Ltotal. For 50 km of fibre, fmax ≈ 2 kHz. Interrogating a longer cable literally costs you bandwidth — a real trade for regional versus local monitoring.

02Reading the data

Waterfalls, directional nulls, and the artefacts that fool everyone once

DAS data is a 2-D matrix — channel against time — and it is normally looked at as a waterfall plot, where a wave sweeping down the cable becomes a sloped stripe whose gradient is its apparent velocity. Moveout you would have inferred from a handful of stations is simply visible.

The catch is directionality. A straight cable is blind in specific directions, and the blindness is severe rather than a mild taper.

The artefacts everyone meets

  • You do not know where your channels are. Cable length ≠ ground distance: slack coils sit in every splice enclosure. Channels are mapped to positions by a tap test — striking the ground at surveyed points and finding which channel lights up. Budget a day for it.
  • Common-mode laser noise appears across all channels at once. It looks like a horizontal stripe on the waterfall and removes cleanly with a median or common-mode subtraction.
  • Everything anthropogenic is recorded. Traffic, trains, pumps, footsteps. On a rail corridor this cuts both ways: trains are a repeatable, high-SNR source that is excellent for ambient-noise imaging and site response, and a nuisance for earthquake detection.
  • Optical fading gives channel-to-channel amplitude that is random, not geological. Never interpret a single channel's amplitude as a site effect without checking its neighbours.

03Software and formats

Do not write your own reader — the ecosystem exists now

DAS spent a decade in a format swamp: every vendor wrote its own HDF5 layout and SEG-Y variant. Two things fixed it — an FDSN metadata standard, and a Python ecosystem modelled explicitly on Astropy's federation idea.

Check your disk before you download

PubDAS is ~90 TB in total and individual datasets run to terabytes. Pull one short window first, confirm your reader and your decimation, and only then scale up. The habit you build here is exactly the habit a real deployment will demand.

06Getting fibre in the Indo-Burma Ranges

Three separate acquisitions: a span, an interrogator, and permission

A CrossRef search returns no DAS seismology papers for India. That is the opportunity and the problem: there is no precedent to point at, so the first agreement has to be argued from scratch. Both plausible fibre owners are central PSUs, which is better than it sounds — they run formal MoU processes with academic institutions and already sell dark fibre as a product.

OwnerNetworkCouplingWhy it matters here
RailTel ~62,000 km along Indian Railways; dark-fibre leasing is an advertised product Buried — good The Lumding–Badarpur hill section climbs through the Barail range near the Kopili fault zone. Buried cable across a structure you already study.
PowerGrid ~78,000 km of OFC, largely OPGW on transmission towers Aerial — poor Widest reach, worst coupling: optical ground wire hangs on towers and is largely decoupled from ground motion. A fallback where rail does not go.
BSNL / BharatNet The broadest state-owned footprint, including rural Meghalaya and Manipur Mixed The only realistic route into the plateau and hill districts that rail and transmission corridors miss.

The interrogator

Buying is not the normal academic route. The published literature is full of acknowledgements to Silixa (iDAS) and Luna/OptaSense for loaning units for specific campaigns; Febus Optics, Terra15, AP Sensing and Sintela are the other names to write to. A vendor will often trade a two-to-four-week loan for a case study in a new market, and "first DAS deployment in India" is a marketing story none of them currently has.

Two alternatives: piggyback on a group that already owns one and already holds regional permits — the Lamont/Columbia and Singapore EOS teams working in Bangladesh and Myanmar — or collaborate with an Indian photonics group (CSIR-CSIO, IIT photonics departments) that builds phase-OTDR systems. A locally built interrogator will be lower-spec than a commercial unit, but for regional earthquake detection lower-spec may be sufficient, and you keep it.

The constraint that is not technical

Telecom infrastructure near the Myanmar border in Manipur and Nagaland is treated as strategic, and permission difficulty rises sharply east of the Barails. The Assam valley and the Shillong Plateau are the tractable side — which is fortunate, because Kopili and the Dauki front are there. Apply through an institution (IIT Guwahati, CSIR-NEIST Jorhat, NCS/MoES) and frame the request as disaster-risk reduction, not research curiosity; that framing survives an administrative review that "PhD experiment" does not.

If what you want is offshore

For the Bengal margin, polarisation-state and laser-interferometry sensing over existing submarine cables is worth knowing about: it needs no interrogator at the far end, only cooperation from whoever operates the link's transceivers. You get one integrated measurement per cable segment rather than per-metre channels — far coarser than DAS — but it has detected teleseisms across ocean basins, and it is the only route to offshore data that does not require ship time.

07A first month that costs nothing

Sequenced so that every step produces something usable even if the next one stalls

  1. Read Lindsey & Martin (2021) end to end. Nothing else makes sense before it.
  2. Install DASCore. Load one short Ridgecrest window from the SCEDC public bucket and produce a waterfall plot of a single aftershock. This is the afternoon that makes DAS concrete.
  3. Run your existing picker on that DAS window and watch it underperform. Understanding why strain data breaks a velocity-trained model is the insight that connects DAS to gap 02 on your ranked list.
  4. Reproduce one figure from Ajo-Franklin (2019) — the PDF is already on your disk — using public data. A reproduction is a legitimate methods note and an unarguable credential.
  5. Write a two-page pilot proposal: a few kilometres of campus dark strand, a loaned interrogator, two weeks, one figure showing a regional Indo-Burma earthquake across a few thousand channels. Send it to three vendors and your institute's networking group in the same week.
  6. Only then approach RailTel — carrying the campus figure. The trunk-line conversation is a different conversation once you can show a recording.
