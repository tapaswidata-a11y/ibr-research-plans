Methods proven elsewhere, not yet tried here

17 approaches that are standard practice on other structurally complex margins — Cascadia, Nankai, Hikurangi, Sumatra, California, the Himalayan front — and that the 895 entries above show have not been applied to the Indo-Burma Ranges. Each is anchored to where it was proven, most often to a method paper already sitting in your own library. 10 of them need nothing but the archive you already hold. The three tracks — seismology, AI/ML and hazard — are now interleaved by rank rather than grouped, and each card carries its track as a chip.

They are ranked 1–17, most startable first. The order is a judgement, not a measurement. Each opening is scored on what could begin with the archive and the one GPU you already have, how novel the regional result would be, and how much the answer would matter — with ties broken towards work that unlocks the items below it. It assumes no field budget and no ship time. Ranks 14–17 are held back by money, permits and partnerships rather than by science, so tell me your actual compute, network access and funding and the order will change.

Start here — a concrete first experiment

Rank 01, and it needs nothing you do not already hold. Take the merged ISC / NEIC / IMD catalogue for 22–28° N, 90–96° E, 1964–2025. Re-estimate Mc and b with the b-positive estimator of van der Elst (2021), then run the b-significant test of Mirwald et al. (2024) — both papers are already in your library — against the spatial and temporal b-variations claimed in the 28 b-value papers listed below. Report which of those claimed anomalies survive a significance test and which do not.

No new data, no GPU, no permissions, and no dependency on anything else on this list. It is a Short Note in length, it corrects the method of your own subfield rather than adding another application of it, and it leaves you with the completeness and declustering machinery that ranks 07 and 08 need anyway.

Modern b-value estimators with significance testing

AI / MLNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
Laptop-scale, published catalogues only, and it lands squarely inside your own thesis topic. Mirwald et al. (2024) is already in your library and the 28 regional b-value papers in this bibliography are the ready-made test set. The most result per week of work on this list.
Proven elsewhere
Mizrahi et al. (2024, SRL) — in your library — showed that most published spatial b-value variations fail a significance test, and introduced b-positive and the b-significant framework.
Why it fits here
Every one of the 28 b-value papers in this bibliography uses maximum-curvature or MLE with no significance testing. Re-analysing the NE India b-value literature with these estimators is a direct, publishable methodological correction of your own field.
What you would need
Published catalogues only. This is a laptop project with a high citation ceiling.

Matched-filter / template-matching catalogue extension

SeismologyNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
Runs on the same archive using your existing catalogue as templates, needs CPU rather than GPU, and gives an independent cross-check on the deep-learning catalogue instead of a second version of it.
Proven elsewhere
Standard practice in Japan and California for two decades — and in your own library it has been applied to Mars (2026 InSight marsquake catalogue) but never to the Indo-Burma Ranges.
Why it fits here
Kopili and Churachandpur–Mao need dense microseismicity before their fault planes can be drawn. Template matching finds events 1–2 magnitude units below the network threshold using the events you already catalogued as templates.
What you would need
Existing catalogue as templates + continuous data. Pairs naturally with the DL catalogue above.

A search for slow slip and tectonic tremor

SeismologyNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
The highest ceiling here and genuinely first-of-its-kind — but a null result is a real possibility, so it ranks below work that is certain to yield a paper. Best run alongside 1–3, not instead of them.
Proven elsewhere
Cascadia (Rogers & Dragert 2003), Nankai, Hikurangi and Guerrero all host episodic tremor and slip on locked, shallow-dipping megathrusts.
Why it fits here
Steckler et al. (2016, Nature Geoscience) showed the Indo-Burman megathrust is locked and loading with exactly that geometry. Every comparable margin that has been searched has found ETS. Nobody has searched here — a CrossRef query returns zero regional studies.
What you would need
Continuous GNSS (already recording) for transients, plus broadband envelope cross-correlation for tremor. A genuinely first-of-its-kind result if it exists.
Read these
Rogers et al. 2003Science10.1126/science.1084783 Obara 2002Science10.1126/science.1070378 Wallace et al. 2016Science10.1126/science.aaf2349
05

Ambient-noise monitoring of velocity change (dv/v)

SeismologyNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
Retrospective, archive-only and low-risk: it works on data already recorded. The monsoon-loading and Kopili pairing is half-written in the regional literature already.
Proven elsewhere
Brenguier and co-workers established dv/v monitoring at Parkfield and Merapi; Lecocq et al. (2017), in your library, used it to track groundwater storage over 30 years.
Why it fits here
Assam's monsoon hydrological loading and the Kopili fault are an ideal pairing — seasonal pore-pressure modulation of seismicity is measurable, and no one has tried it in the region.
What you would need
Two or more continuously recording broadbands and a noise-correlation pipeline. Retrospective — works on archived data.

Machine learning for ionospheric TEC anomaly detection

AI / MLNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
Public data and laptop-scale, and honest false-alarm accounting would be a genuine correction — but the precursor field itself is contested, which caps how far the result travels.
Proven elsewhere
Global seismo-ionospheric work has moved to LSTM and autoencoder anomaly detection with proper false-alarm accounting.
Why it fits here
All eight TEC papers here still use fixed 2σ thresholds over a sliding window. That method cannot quantify its own false-alarm rate — which is precisely the criticism levelled at earthquake-precursor claims. An ML treatment with honest skill scores would be a real contribution.
What you would need
IGS/GNSS TEC archives, all public.

Exposure, fragility and risk — not just hazard

HazardNo regional studyArchive only
Why this rank
No new instruments needed in principle, yet the building inventory is months of survey and remote sensing, and the modelling is structural engineering rather than seismology.
Proven elsewhere
Nath and co-workers built a full vulnerability and risk model for Kolkata (2015, in your library).
Why it fits here
The NE India literature stops at hazard: PGA maps and source zones. No comparable exposure inventory or fragility-based loss model exists for Guwahati, Imphal, Shillong or Silchar, so the hazard numbers never convert into decisions.
What you would need
Building inventories (remote sensing plus survey) and published fragility curves.

A 3-D basin velocity model and physics-based ground-motion simulation

HazardPartially doneNeeds HPC
Why this rank
High value and directly hazard-relevant, but constructing the velocity model is itself a multi-year project before a single simulation runs.
Proven elsewhere
SCEC's CyberShake for California and NIED's work for the Kanto basin simulate ground motion from 3-D structure rather than empirical attenuation.
Why it fits here
Two broadband simulation papers exist for NE India, but there is no 3-D velocity model of the Assam valley or the Sylhet trough — among the deepest sediment basins on Earth. Empirical GMPEs cannot capture basin resonance at those thicknesses; Guwahati, Silchar and Sylhet sit on it.
What you would need
Receiver functions, gravity and existing well logs to build the model; HPC for the simulation.
Read these
Graves et al. 2010Pure and Applied Geophysics10.1007/s00024-010-0161-6

Full-waveform / adjoint tomography

SeismologyNo regional studyNeeds HPC
Why this rank
The science case is strong — slab geometry below 150 km is still argued over — but the HPC allocation is the gate, and the groups competing already have one.
Proven elsewhere
Now routine for Japan, Europe and North America (Fichtner, Tape and successors).
Why it fits here
Every tomographic image of the Indo-Burma slab to date is ray-based travel-time inversion. Slab geometry below ~150 km is still argued over — Rao & Kumar's cessation hypothesis versus continued subduction. Waveform inversion resolves what ray theory smears.
What you would need
Waveform archive plus HPC time. The bottleneck is compute, not data.
Read these
Tape et al. 2009Science10.1126/science.1175298 Fichtner et al. 2009Geophysical Journal International10.1111/j.1365-246x.2009.04368.x

SeismologyOne regional studyField campaign
Why this rank
A funded field season and 100–300 rented nodes. Excellent second-half-of-PhD work once money exists; impossible before it.
Proven elsewhere
Thousands-of-node deployments are now routine in California and Oklahoma. Regionally there is exactly one — a 2025 site-amplification study in Yangon.
Why it fits here
Three-to-six-week nodal deployments across the Kopili fault, the Dauki front or the Shillong Plateau margin would give basin depth, fault location and site response at a spatial density no permanent network can reach.
What you would need
100–300 rentable nodes and a few weeks of fieldwork. No vaults, no telemetry, batteries only.

16

Palaeoseismic trenching on the fold-belt faults

HazardPartially doneField campaign
Why this rank
The only route to pre-instrumental recurrence on Kopili and Churachandpur–Mao, but it is permits, field seasons, radiocarbon budgets and a different discipline.
Proven elsewhere
The Himalayan front has a dense trenching record establishing recurrence for great earthquakes.
Why it fits here
Shillong and Dauki have some palaeoseismic control (Bilham & England 1999; Sukhija 1999), but the Churachandpur–Mao and Kopili faults — the structures now shown to be creeping and seismically active — have no trench-derived recurrence at all.
What you would need
Field seasons, trenching permits and radiocarbon dating. Slow, but it is the only source of pre-instrumental recurrence.
Read these
Bilham et al. 2001Nature10.1038/35071057 Sukhija et al. 1999Earth and Planetary Science Letters10.1016/s0012-821x(99)00015-1
17

Ocean-bottom seismometers on the Rakhine–Bengal margin

SeismologyNo regional studyMajor infrastructure
Why this rank
The most consequential item on the list and the least reachable alone: ship time, an OBS pool and an international consortium. A proposal to join, not to lead.
Proven elsewhere
Cascadia, Hikurangi and Sumatra all resolved their updip megathrust only after going offshore.
Why it fits here
Cummins (2007, Nature) argued the northern Bay of Bengal can host a giant tsunamigenic earthquake. The updip, offshore part of that megathrust — the part that would generate the tsunami — is sampled by no instrument. Onshore networks cannot resolve it.
What you would need
A ship, an OBS pool and international collaboration. The most expensive item here and the most consequential.
Read these
Cummins 2007Nature10.1038/nature06088 Wallace et al. 2016Science10.1126/science.aaf2349
